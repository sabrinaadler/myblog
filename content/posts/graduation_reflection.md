I officially graduated from Penn State with my MS in Computer Science and Engineering, my BS in Computer Science, and my BS in Mathematics. I wanted to take a moment to reflect on my journey at Penn State.

Before college, I thought choosing a major would mean I had to focus on one thing and sacrifice all my other interests. I loved math, chemistry, and physics, but I also loved engineering and building things. In high school, I participated in the NASA HUNCH program where I got the chance to build all sorts of cool things, from a pod to grow little plants in on the ISS to a radiation protection garment. Outside of school, I loved birding, hiking, and being outdoors. These interests did not feel disconnected to me, but I had no idea how I would fit all of them into a single major. I settled on Computer Science because I thought I could bridge the engineering and sciences interests, but I still wasn't sure what I wanted to become. I just knew the field was broad enough to allow me to explore. 

![Picture of me in high school visiting Johnson Space Center to present my HUNCH project and LOVING it!](/myblog/images/graduation/houstan.png)

My freshman year was incredibly challenging for me. I moved three hours away from all of my family and friends, and I felt directionless. The Schreyer Honors College was super helpful for my adjustment. But, I also felt a sense of imposter syndrome: I felt like everyone was so motivated, intelligent, and knew exactly what they wanted to do, and I was still trying to figure things out. Looking back, I know that almost everyone else was also trying to figure it out too, but at the time I thought I was behind. I took an entomology class my first semester because I thought it would be fun and interesting, and I ended up really enjoying it. I contacted the professor, and he put me in touch with Dr. Sara Hermann. I joined the Hermann Lab my spring semester, not sure how it connected to the Computer Science curriculum, but I was just curious about it. I conducted research on aphid interactions with lady beetles; I was fascinated by how much complexity existed within such tiny organisms, and I loved doing research. 

In Spring 2023, I received an offer to join the Applied Research Lab at Penn State as a SOAR Intern; my role would be doing research on Large Language Models. It was only a few months after the public release of ChatGPT, and AI felt like it was suddenly exploding. Before that internship, I had not spent a lot of time thinking about natural language processing or generative AI. That summer, I worked on summarization and translation systems using task-specific language models, and it was the first time I experienced what it was like to work with AI. I found myself scrolling endless blogs, reading academic papers, watching YouTube videos, absorbing whatever I could in my free time to learn more about AI and NLP. I loved the combination of experimentation, engineering, and problem-solving. AI sat at the intersection of so many things that I enjoyed: mathematics, creative thinking, and computer science. I continued working for the ARL throughout my sophomore year, my sophomore summer, and the fall of my junior year, shifting my focus to retrieval-augmented generation systems and applied NLP. Looking back, that internship experience shaped my college experience. I am incredibly grateful for my mentors as well as my peers at the Applied Research Lab for fostering my curiosity and helping me through several learning curves. 

During my sophomore year, I learned about the Integrated Undergraduate Graduate (IUG) Program through the Schreyer Honors College. I was hesitant because I still did not fully know what I wanted to do long term, just that I enjoyed AI and I wanted to connect it to my love of nature. At the same time, the undergraduate coursework did not have what I needed to explore these interests deeper, and I found myself more and more interested in graduate-level courses. Again, I felt a sense of imposter syndrome. I wanted to do it, but I doubted myself too. What if it would be too hard? What if I failed? 

One day, I stumbled on an article about the [INSECT NET](https://insectnet.psu.edu/) program, a National Science Foundation Research Traineeship grant to conduct interdisciplinary research across Entomology, Computer Science, Mechanical Engineering, and other related fields. Reading about the program felt somewhat personal, as though someone had taken all of the interests I had spent years trying to reconcile and built a research community around them. I knew immediately that it was something I wanted to be part of, even if I was not entirely sure how to get involved.

I reached out to Dr. Hermann, whose graduate student was involved in the program, and she encouraged me to take the graduate course "Tech and AI in Living Systems". That course ended up changing the way I thought about both research and my academic path. Until then, my interests had felt like separate paths, a wide chasm between them. But, when I took that course, I met students and faculty that were actively building connections between the fields. They were applying computer vision to ecological monitoring, designing robotic systems inspired by insects, and using machine learning to study biological systems. For the first time, interdisciplinary work stopped feeling abstract and started to feel like something I could contribute to. By the end of the fall semester of my junior year, I realized I wanted to work at the intersection of ecology and computer science. To do this, I would need to fully commit to the IUG; while I still was hesitant and had doubts, I had found a purpose.

I met Christina Grozinger, the Program Director of INSECT NET and Director of the Huck Institutes of the Life Sciences, as well as Mehrdad Mahdavi, one of the computer science faculty involved in the program. Through conversations with them, I began thinking more seriously about what interdisciplinary research could actually look like in practice. The more we discussed possible research directions, the more I realized that computer vision and machine learning could be powerful tools for understanding ecological systems at scale.

![The Graduate Research Exhibition, me standing with my poster on PolliSense.](/myblog/images/graduation/pollisense.png)

That eventually led to the development of PolliSense, my master’s thesis project focused on estimating pollinator habitat quality from ground-level imagery using computer vision and machine learning techniques. The project brought together nearly every academic interest I had explored throughout college: ecology, artificial intelligence, mathematics, and environmental monitoring. More importantly, it showed me how rewarding interdisciplinary research can be when different fields actually inform each other rather than just coexisting.

I was interested in PolliSense because I was intrigued by the idea that everyday, ground-level images could contain meaningful ecological signals that are often overlooked. The project combined computer vision and representation learning techniques with ecological reasoning to estimate pollinator habitat quality from imagery. Working on the project required me to genuinely navigate both ecology and computer science at the same time, which was far more challenging than I initially expected.

One of the biggest lessons I learned was that interdisciplinary research is rarely straightforward. Ecological systems do not fit the assumptions that machine learning models often rely on, and many of the problems we encountered had no obvious solution. Progress required experimentation, reading papers far outside my comfort zone, long debugging sessions, and constant iteration. It was frustrating, at times, not knowing how all the pieces would eventually connect together, but it was also so exciting. More than anything, the project reminded me what brought me to both computer science and ecology: the process of observing patterns, asking questions, and slowly building understanding about complex systems.

While much of my academic work focused on research and experimentation, my internship at Merck showed me what applied machine learning actually looks like in industry. I worked on predictive forecasting systems for controlled temperature unit failures, a problem that was grounded in reliability and decision-making. I appreciated seeing how directly machine learning systems could impact real operational workflows. It challenged my assumptions about impactful AI; success in industry is often less about building the most sophisticated model and more about designing systems that are genuinely useful to the people relying on them. The experience broadened my understanding of machine learning beyond NLP and computer vision and reinforced my interest in applying AI to scientific and real-world problems. 

At the same time, some of the most meaningful experiences that I've had at Penn State came from the mathematical community I found outside classes, research, and work. Throughout college, I was deeply involved in the Penn State Math Club, serving as Vice President for 2 years and President for 1 year. Mathematics was important for my academic balance; it helped me to slow down and think about the reasoning behind what I was working on. More than anything, though, I simply love mathematics for its own sake. I love sitting in lectures and listening to people discuss ideas that I (sometimes) only partially understand, knowing that many of these concepts are the result of centuries of human thought and curiosity. There is something deeply inspiring to me about mathematics as a historical discipline and the way mathematical knowledge continues to build across generations. Even when a topic feels difficult or abstract, I enjoy the process of trying to follow the logic and seeing how different areas of mathematics connect together.

One of the projects that I am most proud of was launching the [Mathematics Directed Reading Program (DRP) at Penn State]({{< ref "posts/building_a_math_directed_reading_program.md" >}}), a mentorship program that pairs mentees (typically undergraduate or early graduate students) with mentors (graduate students or faculty) to explore mathematical topics outside the standard curriculum. You can read more about that in my previous blog post if you're curious! Beyond this, Math Club gave me many of the friendships that defined my college experience. 

College also changed me in much smaller and more personal ways that are harder to capture on a resume. I learned how to cook and bake, kept a sourdough starter named Phineas alive far longer than I expected, started experimenting more with art, and slowly became more comfortable creating things purely because I enjoyed them. I learned how to manage money, schedule appointments, navigate healthcare, and handle all the small responsibilities that come with adulthood and independence. None of these moments felt particularly significant while they were happening, but together they fundamentally changed my confidence and sense of self-sufficiency.

![I enjoyed birding in Alaska and seeing so many lifers](/myblog/images/graduation/alaska.png)

I also had the opportunity to travel far more than I ever imagined before college. Over the past four years, I visited places like Germany, Costa Rica, Iceland, Mexico, and Alaska, experiences that expanded my perspective far beyond academics and research. I will be back in Germany soon, which I will hopefully write a blog post about, and I will also be giving a talk at the [International Conference on Pollinator Biology, Health and Policy](https://plantsciences.montana.edu/pollinatorconference2026/index.html) at Montana State University in a little over a month. 

I entered college unsure of myself and nervous that I made a wrong decision. I wasn't sure how to honor all of my interests in my studies, and I felt like I would have to give up parts of myself to succeed. I had expected classwork to be different in college, but I didn't really imagine what it would actually be like. Looking back now, I think that I was worried about the wrong things. I worried so much about my major and the coursework, but the experiences that actually shaped me most were my research, internships, clubs, friends, and hobbies. I think what surprised me most was how difficult it would be to strike a balance between everything. My workload was rarely stable; some weeks I had multiple projects, homeworks, research deadlines, and more that I needed to complete, and others were less packed. I had to learn to be proactive and start things much earlier than I had to in high school, and time management became much more crucial. 

The biggest thing I learned was how important and impactful interdisciplinary work was; before college, I thought careers were more siloed into a single discipline, but now I have learned how much collaboration happens in both research and industry. Skills like being an adept listener and communicating in understandable terms became more critical than I had previously thought. I had believed my interests would be challenging to connect, but now I can see all the connections. I have come to love interdisciplinary work precisely because of the additional challenges that it poses. 

For me, college has led me to develop a deeper understanding of a few fields instead of narrowing myself entirely into one. More importantly, though, it taught me that the most meaningful work often happens at the intersections between disciplines. Some of the most rewarding experiences that I had at Penn State came from being surrounded by people with completely different backgrounds and perspectives who all approached problems in different ways. Those experiences pushed me to become more comfortable asking questions, learning outside my comfort zone, and admitting when I did not know something.

I also learned that curiosity is far more valuable than I realized when I first entered college. Coming into Penn State, I worried that having too many interests meant I lacked direction. I thought successful people had a perfectly linear plan from the beginning. Instead, I found that many of the opportunities that shaped my college experience came from following curiosity into spaces where I initially felt underqualified or uncertain. Some of the most important moments of the past four years happened because I took a class that seemed interesting, emailed someone whose work fascinated me, or joined a project without fully knowing where it would lead.

I know that I want to continue working on problems that connect disciplines, combine technical and scientific thinking, and contribute to areas that I genuinely care about. That is part of what excites me about beginning Merck’s Emerging Talent Rotation (ETR) program, where I will continue working on AI and machine learning systems in applied scientific settings. More than anything, though, I am excited by the opportunity to keep learning. Whether my path eventually leads me deeper into machine learning for ecology, scientific AI systems, or something entirely different years from now, I hope to continue building at the intersections between fields that first inspired me during college.

When I entered college, I thought I would eventually need to choose between my interests. Instead, Penn State taught me how to connect them. Thank you to everyone who has helped me along my journey. We Are.
