Building a Math Directed Reading Program

Reflections on launching Penn State's Math Directed Reading Program.
March 9, 2026 · 8 min · Sabrina Adler

Over the past year, I have been working alongside my fellow Penn State undergraduate students Daniel Harmon, Prajwal Bhandari, and Samarth Khandelwal as well as Penn State graduate students Nick Payne and Jake Canel on Penn State’s first Mathematics Directed Reading Program (DRP). A DRP pairs undergraduate students with mentors for a semester-long guided reading on an advanced mathematical topic. The format is simple: students meet with their mentor weekly, work through papers or textbook chapters, and present their work at the end of the semester.

Programs like this exist at several universities, but Penn State did not have one previously (to our knowledge). This post reflects on what it took to build the program, what worked well (and what didn’t), and my main takeaways.

What is a Directed Reading Program?#

A directed reading program occupies a niche between an independent study and a reading course.

The topics included in the DRP typically lie outside the standard undergraduate curriculum. Some of the topics that have run so far include:

  • Differential geometry
  • Dynamical systems
  • Fourier analysis
  • Mathematical biology
  • Number theory

Each student is paired with a mentor (either a graduate student or a faculty member) who helps guide the reading, clarify concepts, and aid learning.

The goal of the program is not just to learn the subject, but discover how professional mathematicians learn mathematics. Graduate students and professors often learn mathematics through reading, discussing material, and thinking deeply about concepts.

Our Motivation#

We had three main motivations for establishing this DRP.

First, we noticed a significant gap between undergraduate coursework and traditional reading courses or graduate-level study. Undergraduate courses are typically highly structured, with lecture notes, problem sets, and clearly defined expectations. They rarely require students to learn material independently from a textbook. In contrast, reading courses, which are often the next step for interested students, require a very different skill set. Students are expected to read advanced texts, guide their own learning, and approach faculty members directly to propose a topic. For many undergraduates, this transition can feel abrupt and intimidating. Our goal was to soften this gap by creating a structured program that matches students with mentors. Within this framework, mentors can help students develop the skills needed to read mathematics independently and gradually prepare them for more traditional reading courses.

Second, we observed that many students had mathematical interests that extended beyond the scope of the standard undergraduate curriculum. While courses introduce foundational topics, they rarely allow students to explore specialized areas in depth. For instance, a student might develop an interest in a specific topic within topology, but a traditional topology course cannot always accomodate a deeper exploration of that niche area. The DRP allows for much greater flexibility. Because the reading groups are small and collaborative, students and mentors can adjust the pace and direction of the material. If a group wants to spend additional time on a particularly interesting topic, the format makes that possible in a way that traditional classes often cannot.

Third, we recognized that undergraduate and graduate students in mathematics had relatively few opportunities to interact. We wanted to create a space for structured mentorship that could strengthen connections within the department. For undergraduates, the program provides guidance on topics such as graduate school, research, and mathematical career paths. For graduate students, mentoring offers the opportunity to share their experience, develop teaching and advising skills, and build relationships with younger members of the mathematical community.

Designing the Program#

Starting a program from scratch required building several pieces of infrastructure to ensure the program’s success.

Applications#

Fall 2025#

In Fall 2025, we designed two applications. One was for undergraduate students where they could list mathematical areas they were interested in, courses they have taken, any previous experiences with independent reading, and more. The other was for graduate students which asked how many students they would like to mentor, the topic areas they were interested in, and more.

Matching students turned out to be the most challenging part. First, we had many more undergraduate students sign up than we had expected; we expected that around 15 students would sign up, but we had ~35 students sign up. Second, many undergraduate students were interested in the same topic areas, but we would only have 1 or 2 graduate students in those areas, which meant that we had to match students to mentors that did not have similar interests.

Our main feedback were about the matching process. We prioritzed placing undergraduates in groups that aligned with their interests, but there still ended up being some groups with misaligned interests. Additionally, because of this prioritization, an unexpected consequence was that some mentors had students with widely different mathematical backgrounds, which made leading the group very difficult. This led us to make many changes for Spring 2026.

Spring 2026#

Similarly, we had two applications again. The undergraduate form was much less in depth and did not ask about mathematical interests. The graduate form was the same. We held a matching event where we stratified mentors into three groups: beginners (background: no proofs and intro calculus), intermediate (background: some proofs + analysis), and advanced (background: strong proofs + analysis + taken 400 level courses). Undergraduate students were asked to decide which level they were (mostly by coursework), and then talk to each of the mentors in that level. Then, they could pick their top 5 choices.

This matching process worked much better. Mentors ended up with groups composed of undergraduates that had a similar background. Also, students had more varied top choices than in Fall 2025. I think that conversations allowed mentees to see which mentors would be a better fit.

Structure#

Each reading group had their own structure, but we provided some general guidelines such as

  • weekly meetings
  • assigned readings in between meetings
  • final presentation or paper at the end of the semester

The Symposium#

For Fall 2025, we organized a small symposium with a panel of mentees to talk about their experience and get feedback. We did not have time to organize talks, so we did this instead. For Spring 2026, we are planning to do presentations or papers for the final symposium

What worked well#

I was surprised about how well the program took off.

General Enthusiasm#

We were shocked by the amount of enthusiasm that this program was met with from undergraduates, graduates, and faculty. For both semesters we had about 35 undergraduates and 15 graduate/faculty mentors. I had only expected around 15-20 undergraduates to be interested. Also, we had a lot of interest from graduate students, and even some faculty wanted to mentor as well.

The Community Aspect#

One benefit was the community that grew around the program. In Fall 2025, we got a lounge dedicated for the Math Club, and a community began to grow there, centered around the DRP. Mentees would meet to do their readings and talk before they met with their mentors.

Challenges#

The Matching Process#

As outlined in applications, we found the matching process to be the most difficult aspect, especially in the fall when we relied on a single form. Since it was the beginning of the semester, I did not know many of the mentees who had signed up, so I could not rely on anything other than what they submitted on the form. It was hard to know exactly what people were looking for out of the program, especially because I made some open ended questions on the form optional. Therefore, I think we ended up with a few groups with poor matches. But, I think it was also somewhat beneficial because it allowed students to explore areas of math they would have not explored otherwise.

Organization#

I found it to be somewhat straightforward to organize, but it still required quite a bit of attention on top of everything else with the Math Club. Between the new lounge, the start of the DRP, the undergraduate talks, John Urschel’s visit, and the regular GBMs, I always felt like there was something that needed to get done. Therefore, next year the Math Club will have a dedicated chair position for the DRP who can focus much more energy into the program than I could have.

Textbooks & Funding#

We did not have funding for the DRP program for the 2025-26 school year since this was a “proof of concept” program. We wanted to be able to supply some textbooks, but due to funding restrictions, we could not. That is why I am starting a program to collect textbook donations from faculty and graduate students.

The Impact#

I am beyond proud of this program and what we have started. Other than the lounge, this program was my final major contribution to the Math Club. I believe that it is helping to fill a previously ignored gap in the undergraduate population, and I wish that I had a program like this when I was younger.

Looking forward#

The first year of the program was an experiment, but the response from students and mentors suggests that it fills an important role in the department.

Going forward, we hope to:

  • expand the number of reading groups
  • develop clearer topic guides for new participants
  • build stronger connections between undergraduate and graduate communities

Most importantly, we hope the program continues to give students the opportunity to explore mathematics beyond the classroom.

Starting the DRP was one of the most rewarding projects I have worked on during my time at Penn State, and I am excited to see how it changes and evolves!
